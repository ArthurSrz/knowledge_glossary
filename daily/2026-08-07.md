The last episode proved we could actually steer or drive a [[large language model]] using activation chains, leaving one question open : how do we define with [[knowledge]] where we want the [[model]] to go ? For steering is useless if we can't define where we want the [[model]] to go. 

There is an old and popular idea that we aim at what we are rewarded for. Some psychologist have claimed that human are driven through a "reinforcement by reward" mechanism: that even when we act with no external inducements, we are still driven by an inner reward signal that comes from inside ourselves. 

The problem with this idea is that when we strive for hard and complex problems solution, we encounter a deadly circularity : **you first must be able to do something before you can be rewarded for doing it !** The steering can't come from reward because we don't even know what the basis for reward is. This circularity was no great problem when Ivan Pavlov studied conditioned reflexes nearly a century ago, because in his experiments the animals never needed to produce new kinds of behavior; they only had to link new stimuli to old behaviors. Decades later Pavlov's research was extended by the Harvard psychologist B. F. Skiller, who recognized that higher animals did indeed sometimes exhibit new forms of bevavior, which he called *operants*. Skinner's experiments confirmed that when a certain operant is followed by a reward, any kind of reward, it is likely to reappear more frequently on later occasions. He also discovered that this kind of learning has much larger effects *if the animal can not predict when it will be rewarded*. Under names like *operant conditioning* and *behavior modification*, Skinner's discoveries had a wide influence in psychology and education, but never led to explain how brains produce new *operant* at first. Furthermore, few of these animal experiments shed much light on how humans learn to form and carry out their complex plans to [[reach]] unobserved [[goals]]; the trouble is that other animals can scarcely lean such things at all. Those twin ideas (reward/success and punish/failure) do not explain enough about how people manage to produce new ideas and plans to steer a long-lasting project that should [[reach]] a solution. Let's take for example the engineers that were in charge during WWII to imagine and build "fabricated ports" to unload several hundred of thousand men and tons of equipment to support the liberation of Europe : they had no basis for any reward, and count not solve the issue with lifetimes of ineffectual trial and errors. 

The answer must lie in learning better ways to steer. In order to discuss these things, we'll have to start by using many ordinary words like *goal*, *reward*, *learning*, *thinking*, *recognizing*, *liking*, *wanting*, *imagining* and *remembering*: all based on old and vague ideas. We'll find that most such words must be replaced by new distinctions and ideas. Still, there is something common to them all: in order to solve any hard problem, we must use various kind of memories. At each moment, **we must keep track** of what we've just done or we'll end up doing pointless things. Finally, once our problem is solved, we need access to records of how it was done, for use when similar problem arise in the furutre. 

Much of [[The Society of Knowledge]] has in some way been concerned with [[memory]] (that is, with records of the mental past, expressed through [[knowledge]] artifacts). Why, how, and when should such records be made? When the human brain solves a hard problem, many millions of [[agent]]s (not talking about AI agents, just agent) and [[process]]es are involved, and dozens if not hundreds of artifacts were built or consumed.
