# We want what maximizes a reward but is there always a reward ? 

There is an old and popular idea that we aim at what we are rewarded for. Some psychologist have claimed that human are driven through a "reinforcement by reward" mechanism: that even when we act with no external inducements, we are still looking for an inner reward signal that comes from inside ourselves. 

The problem with this idea is that when we strive for hard and complex problems solution, we encounter a deadly circularity : **you first must be able to do something before you can imagine a reward for doing it !** On complex problems, we can't look for reward because we don't even know what the basis for reward is. This circularity was no great problem when Ivan Pavlov studied conditioned reflexes nearly a century ago, because in his experiments the animals never needed to produce new kinds of behavior; they only had to link new stimuli to old behaviors. Decades later Pavlov's research was extended by the Harvard psychologist B. F. Skiller, who recognized that higher animals did indeed sometimes exhibit new forms of behavior, which he called *operants*. Skinner's experiments confirmed that when a certain operant is followed by a reward, any kind of reward, it is likely to reappear more frequently on later occasions. He also discovered that this kind of learning has much larger effects *if the animal can not predict when it will be rewarded*. Under names like *operant conditioning* and *behavior modification*, Skinner's discoveries had a wide influence in psychology and education, but never led to explain how brains produce new *operant* and purpose to encourage the production of operants. Furthermore, few of these animal experiments shed much light on how humans learn to form and carry out their complex plans to [[reach]] unobserved [[goals]]; the trouble is that other animals can scarcely learn such things at all. 

# The problem of problems with no precedent

Those twin ideas (reward/success and punish/failure) do not explain enough about how people manage to produce new ideas and plans to steer a long-lasting project that should [[reach]] a solution. Let's take for example the engineers that were in charge during WWII to imagine and build "fabricated ports" to unload several hundred of thousand men and tons of equipment to support the liberation of Europe : they had no basis for any reward as fabricated ports had never been built; neither could they solve the issue with lifetimes of ineffectual trial and errors as they only had 12 to 18 months to find a solution.

# Keeping track as a non-negotiable

The answer must lie in learning better ways to steer and [[reach]] [[goals]]. In order to discuss these things, we'll have to start by using many ordinary words like *goal*, *reward*, *learning*, *thinking*, *recognizing*, *liking*, *wanting*, *imagining* and *remembering*: all based on old and vague ideas. We'll find that most such words must be replaced by new distinctions and ideas. Still, there is something common to them all: in order to solve any hard problem, [[reach]] [[goals]], we must use various kind of memories. At each moment, **we must keep track** **of what we've just done or we'll end up doing pointless things**. Finally, once our problem is solved, we need access to records of how it was done, for use when similar problem arise in the future

Much of [[The Society of Knowledge]] has in some way been concerned with [[memory]] (that is, with records of the mental past, expressed through [[knowledge]] artifacts). Why, how, and when should such records be made? When the human brain solves a hard problem, many millions of [[agent]]s (not talking about [[AI agent]], just [[agent]]) and [[process]]es are involved, and dozens if not hundreds of artifacts are built or consumed. Which [[agent]] could be wise enough to guess what changes should then be made? what artifacts are missing? The high-level [[agent]] can't know such things; they scarcely know which lower-level processes exists. Nor can lower-level agents know which of their actions helped [[reach]] high-level [[goals]]; they scarcely know that higher-level [[goals]] exist. At a human brain level, the agencies that move our legs aren't concerned with whether we are walking toward home or toward work, nor do the agents involved with such destinations know anything about controlling individual muscle units. 

# How do we decide which piece of [[knowledge]] is to praise for reaching one goal ? 

The same applies for societies of [[knowledge]]: the high-level [[knowledge]] artifacts can't know exactly how to [[reach]] any goal because they don't know which lower-level artifacts are necessary. Nor can lower-level artifact say which of their components can help achieve high-level [[goals]], because they don't know the higher-level [[goals]] exist. The laws that govern a country for example aren't concerned with whether a particular clerk stamps a particular form on a particular Tuesday, and the clerk who stamps it has no idea which [[principle]] of law is being enacted. Each part knows only a narrow band of levels above and below its own.

So where in societies of [[knowledge]] are judgements made about which artifacts merit praise or blame depending on if they help achieve their goal ?
